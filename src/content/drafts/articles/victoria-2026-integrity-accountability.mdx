[Part five](/articles/victoria-2026-integrity-findings) sorted the investigations by what each one actually found. This is the other half of that question, and the one that decides a vote: what would change. A finding tells you what went wrong once. The powers, the funding and the disclosure rules tell you whether it gets found next time.

There is a reason to write this now rather than after the election. The biggest expansion of IBAC's jurisdiction since it was created in 2012 is in front of the Legislative Council as I write, and it may or may not become law before the campaign starts. What it does, and what was added to it at the last minute, is the sharpest test available of how this government handles scrutiny of itself.

<h2 id="the-same-problem" tabindex="-1">Start with the same problem</h2>

Three constraints explain most of what frustrated me in [part five](/articles/victoria-2026-integrity-findings). IBAC could only investigate conduct that would amount to a criminal offence, which is why Operation Daintree found improper political influence in a procurement and still made no corrupt-conduct finding. It could only hold public hearings in "exceptional circumstances", a phrase the courts read as rare and unusual, and one with no equivalent in New South Wales. And its commissioners are appointed through committees the government dominates <Cite k="cpi-ibac-reform" />. That analysis is from 2022, so it describes the problem the current bill is meant to fix rather than the law today, but the shape of it held all the way through the findings I read.

Then there's money. In February, IBAC, the Ombudsman and the Auditor-General jointly asked for something unglamorous: a transparent funding process. Written explanations of budget decisions, the ability to brief parliamentary oversight committees before those decisions are made, exemption from efficiency dividends, a published protocol. The Ombudsman's description of the present arrangement was that it is "opaque and shrouded in secrecy" <Cite k="integrity-agencies-funding" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. A government that sets its own watchdogs' budgets, without explaining how, holds a quiet lever over how much they can look into. No party has answered that ask, and it's the one I'd most like answered.

Two more pieces of the architecture. Political donations were re-regulated in June, after a High Court decision knocked out the old arrangements: a $7,500 cap per election period, 21-day disclosure, more public funding. The Centre for Public Integrity calls it "an emergency repair job, not a long-term solution" <Cite k="cpi-political-finance" />. And freedom of information is under load rather than under repair: Victorians made 58,181 FOI requests in 2024–25, up 9.4% and another record <Cite k="ovic-annual-2024-25" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />, which tells you how much people are having to ask for and nothing about how well they're answered.

<h2 id="what-the-government-has-done" tabindex="-1">What the government has done in seven weeks</h2>

I want to give this its full weight before I get to the problem with it, because the substance is real.

On 29 July, four days into the job, Carroll called the royal commission into construction that Jacinta Allan had refused, promising "exhaustive terms of reference" across contractors, subcontractors, labour hire, government and unions <Cite k="abc-royal-commission-july" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. Christopher Kourakis was appointed on 20 August and reports in August 2027 <Cite k="abc-kourakis" />. On 8 September the government legislated to strengthen it: Victoria's first Office of the Special Prosecutor with authority to prosecute, power for royal commissions to compel written statements outside hearings with non-compliance a criminal offence, and evidence-sharing with police, IBAC and regulators <Cite k={["premier-rc-powers", "mandarin-corruption-bills"]} /> <Tag kind="promise" />.

The same day it introduced the IBAC bill. On the law-firm reading, it extends IBAC's jurisdiction to "associated entities" — contractors, subcontractors, agents, joint-venture partners, anyone performing a public function or handling public money — where before it could only investigate public bodies and public officers. It replaces the criminal-offence threshold with "any criminal offence or a serious disciplinary matter", which is the grey-corruption gap that swallowed Daintree. It clarifies that a benefit need not actually have been obtained <Cite k={["ashurst-ibac-bill", "premier-ibac-powers"]} /> <Tag kind="promise" />. If it passes, it is the most significant change to the watchdog's reach since 2012, and it fixes two of the three structural problems above. It does not touch the public-hearings threshold, and it does not touch funding.

Now the part that has to be in the same section. A late amendment, circulated at six o'clock on the Monday evening, stops IBAC investigating government construction matters that have already been addressed — which covers the organised-crime allegations Allan referred to IBAC two years ago and which IBAC did not take up <Cite k="aap-ibac-carveout" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. The bill's own transitional provisions say the same thing in lawyer's English: the new powers reach back to past conduct, except conduct previously dismissed or completed <Cite k="ashurst-ibac-bill" />. In June, when these powers were first announced, they were described as applying to recent allegations <Cite k="abc-ibac-june-2026" />.

Carroll's answer is that the royal commission covers that ground, so a fresh referral isn't necessary, and that a new referral or new information could still start an investigation; he would not say whether he'd make one <Cite k="aap-ibac-carveout" />. That is a real argument rather than an evasion, and it is not enough. The royal commission reports in August 2027, after this election and after a parliament. A carve-out that happens to exclude the single largest matter the reform was written for is the kind of thing that needs to be explained on the record rather than circulated at 6pm.

For the avoidance of any doubt about where this sits: the bill passed the Assembly on 10 September and is at second reading in the Council <Cite k="ibac-bill-status" />. It is not law. If you're reading this after the election, check that first.

<h2 id="the-alternatives" tabindex="-1">What the alternatives would change</h2>

The Coalition's integrity offer is specific about this scandal and quiet about the institutions. It pressed for a royal commission before the government called one, and Jess Wilson's objection to the one we got is precise and fair: no completion timing, no guaranteed reporting date, no assurance that Labor ministers will be called <Cite k="abc-royal-commission-july" />. Robert Redlich, who ran IBAC, made the same point from the outside — a commission is only as good as its terms of reference, and governments have been known to call one with very limited jurisdiction <Cite k="abc-royal-commission-july" />. The Coalition's institutional commitments sit in its economic plan rather than an integrity policy: a Charter of Budget Honesty, a real-time expenditure tracker, a construction watchdog <Cite k="lib-10-year-plan" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. After a hidden fare levy and a savings claim that covered items never budgeted ([part ten](/articles/victoria-2026-state-budget)), a budget-honesty charter is a better-aimed policy than it sounds.

What I could not find, anywhere, was a Coalition position on IBAC's funding, the public-hearings threshold, the definition of corrupt conduct, commissioner appointments, freedom of information, donations, lobbying or whistleblower protection. A party campaigning hard on corruption has published nothing about the body that investigates it. I'd rather say that plainly than pretend the silence is a policy.

The Greens have the most complete platform and the least power to enact it: fund the integrity bodies so they aren't rationing inquiries, remove the limits on public hearings, ban donations from for-profit businesses and their directors, a five-year cooling-off period before ministers and senior staff can lobby, and strip commercial-in-confidence out of FOI <Cite k="greens-integrity-policy" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. None of it is costed. Their leader's objection to the September bill was about process — a bill handed over at 6pm and voted two days later <Cite k="aap-ibac-carveout" />. On this one I think the process objection is the substantive one.

For One Nation, polling around a fifth of the vote, I found no integrity or anti-corruption policy at all. Given the party's pitch is that the system is rigged against ordinary people, that's a strange hole, and I'll update this if something appears.

Outside the parties, Transparency International wants the commission's terms as broad as possible, including whether the previous government ignored or enabled what was going on, and an assessment of where IBAC and the police fell short <Cite k="ti-royal-commission" />. That last one matters: the reason a royal commission exists at all is partly that the standing watchdog didn't take the referral.

<h2 id="who-bears-the-cost" tabindex="-1">What this is worth to someone out here</h2>

I nearly left this article out of the plan, because integrity can read as a city preoccupation — a fight between lawyers about processes, while the road still has a hole in it. Having done the rest of the series, I think that's exactly backwards, and here's the chain.

The Auditor-General found that the department doesn't always document why it prioritises one road site over another, and that one in four routine works inspected didn't meet the standard ([part two](/articles/victoria-2026-regional-roads)). The emergency-services levy was designed, revised twice and frozen inside six weeks without anyone explaining the basis of the farm rate ([part three](/articles/victoria-2026-emergency-services-levy)). A 1% charge was added to every fare in the state in January 2025 and disclosed to nobody until an auditor found it. The Commonwealth Games business case understated the costs and overstated the benefits, and agencies failed to give frank and timely advice ([part five](/articles/victoria-2026-integrity-findings)). Every one of those is a failure of documentation, disclosure or advice, and every one of them cost regional Victorians money or service.

That's what "competent government" means in this article, and it's why it belongs with the watchdogs rather than in its own box. The bodies that find these things are the Auditor-General, the Ombudsman and IBAC. Their budgets are set by the government they examine, through a process their own heads call opaque. A country voter's stake in integrity policy is not abstract at all: it's whether the next roads program is documented well enough to be audited, and whether anyone can find out.

<h2 id="what-id-need-answered" tabindex="-1">What I'd need answered</h2>

Applying the [five questions](/articles/victoria-election-2026#questions-before-voting). What actually changed: a royal commission exists, a special prosecutor is being created, and IBAC's jurisdiction is a Council vote away from being genuinely wider. Who was responsible: the conduct under investigation belongs to a previous premier, the carve-out belongs to this one, and the constraints that let grey corruption through belong to a parliament that wrote them in 2011. What supports the claim: an auditor, a law firm's reading of the bill, and a wire report of an amendment I could not read myself. Who bears the cost: everyone who relies on a program nobody documented properly. Is the alternative credible: sharper than the government on this scandal, silent on the machinery.

The three questions I'd put to any candidate. Will you support the funding protocol the three watchdogs asked for in February? Will you remove the "exceptional circumstances" limit on public hearings? And on the carve-out: if the Big Build allegations can't go back to IBAC, who examines them before August 2027?

I said in [the first article](/articles/victoria-election-2026) that I wanted a government that accepts scrutiny. Seven weeks of Carroll have produced more real scrutiny machinery than the previous two years, and a 6pm amendment that keeps the biggest matter out of it. Both of those are him. I don't think I get to pick one.

[The state budget](/articles/victoria-2026-state-budget) is the previous part. The people actually on my ballot comes once nominations close on 9 November; the final weighing comes after that.
