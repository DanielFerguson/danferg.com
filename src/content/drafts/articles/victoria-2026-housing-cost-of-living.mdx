Roads and the levy were my grievances. Housing is the one I hear about from other people: the business that can’t find staff because there’s nowhere for them to live, the family paying city rent for a country house, the friend’s kid who has given up on buying anywhere near where they grew up. I don’t rent and I’m not trying to buy, so I owe this topic more care, not less. It’s also the first topic in [the guide](/articles/victoria-election-2026) where the alternatives have put forward enough detail to compare properly.

<h2 id="the-same-problem" tabindex="-1">Start with the same problem</h2>

The official rent series has a regional Victorian median of $470 a week in the September quarter of 2025, up 4.6% in a year, against $580 in Melbourne, up 3.5% <Cite k="hv-rental-report" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />. Regional rents are lower and rising faster. Industry figures for March 2026 put the regional house median at $520 with vacancy at 2.3% <Cite k="reiv-kusher-apr-2026" />, though those are advertised rents rather than bonds, so I don’t put them beside the official number. No 2026 quarter of the official report had been published when I checked, which is itself worth noting in an election year.

The sharper picture is Anglicare’s March snapshot. Of 2,682 regional listings that weekend, 75 were affordable for a household on income support. Three per cent. For a minimum-wage household it was 49%, which sounds better until you remember it means a full-time worker on the minimum couldn’t afford half the houses advertised in country Victoria <Cite k="anglicare-snapshot-2026" /> <Tag kind="independent" />.

On supply, the government set itself a target in 2023 of 800,000 homes in a decade, or 80,000 a year <Cite k="premier-800k" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. Victoria completed 14,519 dwellings in the March quarter of 2026 <Cite k="abs-building-activity-mar-2026" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />, and the Property Council reads the calendar-2025 total as 54,156, down 10% on the year before <Cite k="property-council-2025-completions" />. At that pace the state is building roughly two homes for every three the target requires <Tag kind="author" />. The federal supply council puts Victoria’s completions down 9% over the year and a third of the way through its Housing Accord share <Cite k="nhsac-aug-2026" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. Whoever wins inherits that gap, and none of the promises below closes it on their own.

Then there are the people the private market has already failed. The Victorian Housing Register held 67,261 applications in September 2025, 38,731 of them priority, and again no 2026 figure was published when I looked <Cite k="vhr-sep-2025" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. Homelessness services in Latrobe–Gippsland saw 7,800 people in 2024–25, a rate of 251 per 10,000, and the state’s north-west ran higher still at 283, against a national rate of 106 <Cite k="aihw-shs-2024-25" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. This is not a Melbourne problem that leaks into the regions. On these numbers it is worse here.

<h2 id="labors-record-and-offer" tabindex="-1">What Labor has done, and what it’s offering</h2>

The delivered part of the record is mostly law. Since November 2025 rental bidding is banned, no-fault evictions are gone, rent increases need 90 days’ notice and a property has to meet minimum standards before it’s advertised <Cite k="premier-renters-nov-2025" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. Those rules apply in Mildura the same as in Brunswick. The taskforce that enforces them has inspected more than 600 properties and issued about 130 infringements, and was made permanent in August <Cite k="spi-rental-taskforce" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. I couldn’t find how many of those inspections happened outside Melbourne.

The built part is where the tags matter. The government counts 12,000 Big Housing Build homes “completed or underway” and more than 7,000 finished <Cite k="premier-bhb-apr-2026" /> <Tag kind="govt" />; the 2020 promise was 12,000 by 2026, so the deadline is being met by counting homes still under construction. Freedom-of-information documents show the program was about a billion dollars over its early budget and that cutting 1,100 homes was considered <Cite k="abc-bhb-foi" />. The regional slice is the $1 billion Regional Housing Fund: 1,300 homes promised by 2028, 630 completed or under construction, and of the 377 the page calls completed, 90 are new homes, 72 transitional and 187 are upgrades of houses that already existed <Cite k="hv-regional-housing-fund" /> <Tag kind="funded" />. Count the new dwellings generously, including the transitional ones, and it is under 200. Set that against 7,800 people through Gippsland’s homelessness services in a single year and the scale of the response is clear <Tag kind="author" />. The May budget added $860 million to the growth fund for more than 7,000 social homes with community housing providers, with no regional share stated <Cite k="premier-shgf-apr-2026" /> <Tag kind="funded" />. The peak body’s reading of the Productivity Commission’s numbers is that social housing is 2.88% of Victorian households, the lowest of any state, and that public stock grew by a net 36 dwellings over a decade while community housing carried the growth <Cite k="chp-rogs-2026" />.

On supply, everything the government announced this year is about Melbourne: activity centres, the townhouse code, ten-day approvals for up to four homes on a block <Cite k="premier-townhouses-sep-2026" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. Those are permits, not houses, and none of it touches a regional town. The regional instrument is a set of capacity targets to 2051 that councils must write into their planning schemes, with Geelong at 128,600, Ballarat 46,900, Bendigo 37,000 and Shepparton 15,250 <Cite k="planning-housing-targets" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. A target for a council is not a plan to build anything, and the page attaches no money.

The cost-of-living offer is the May budget: a 20% registration rebate worth up to $186 a car, which matters more in a two-car household forty minutes from the nearest supermarket; half-price public transport to 1 January, which matters less if no service reaches you; about $566 million in concessions; and $47 million across emergency accommodation, long-term housing and vulnerable renters <Cite k="budget-2026-col" /> <Tag kind="funded" />. The Power Saving Bonus round closed in March and nothing has replaced it <Cite k="premier-psb-jan-2026" />. Ben Carroll has been Premier since July and has announced no new housing or cost-of-living spending that I could find, which is consistent with his stated restraint and leaves the campaign resting on the budget.

One decision the government defends rather than advertises: the 2023 land-tax change that cut the tax-free threshold from $300,000 to $50,000 for ten years. The Parliamentary Budget Office estimated it brought about 380,000 new taxpayers in at an average of $878 a year and lifted existing bills by $2,132 on average <Cite k="pbo-land-tax-2024" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. In the country that reaches the holiday house, the block held for a child, and the small landlord whose tenant is the family renting at $470 a week.

<h2 id="the-coalition" tabindex="-1">What the Coalition would change</h2>

The Liberal–National offer is mostly tax. Stamp duty would go for first home buyers on properties up to $1 million, established or off the plan, where the current relief stops at $750,000 <Cite k={["lib-fhb-stamp-duty", "sro-fhb-duty"]} /> <Tag kind="promise" />. The land-tax threshold would go back to $300,000, by $50,000 a year over five years. The short-stay levy and the emergency-services levy would be repealed. Jess Wilson put a figure of $534 a year on household savings in her budget reply, and named scrapping Treaty and a 2% smaller public service as the offsets <Cite k={["wilson-budget-reply-2026", "nats-policies"]} /> <Tag kind="promise" />. I couldn’t find a breakdown of the $534, a published cost for any of the tax measures, or a start date for the stamp-duty change.

On supply, the February plan would clear a backlog of 27 precinct structure plans, expand the Capital City Zone, and hand structure planning back to councils in place of Labor’s activity centres, with a promise to “partner with councils” on regional growth <Cite k="lib-housing-plan-feb-2026" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. In September David Southwick told a developer forum the 70% established-areas target would go and the market would decide the split <Cite k="acm-housing-forum-sep-2026" />. There is no dwelling target and no dollar figure anywhere in the plan. The Nationals add regional planning measures: mandated approval timeframes, clearer cultural-heritage timelines and annual audits of approvals <Cite k="nats-policies" />.

What I searched for and didn’t find: a social housing target, a homelessness policy beyond one local candidate’s pledge, a position on renters’ rights, or anything on the regional first-home-owner grant that ended in 2021 <Cite k="sro-regional-fhog-closed" />. None of the housing or tax items appears in the Coalition’s first-100-days plan either <Cite k="nats-100-days" />, so whatever the timetable is, it isn’t the first hundred days.

<h2 id="the-crossbench" tabindex="-1">The Greens, One Nation and the rest</h2>

The Greens have the most detailed alternative and the most contested one. A two-year rent freeze, then a permanent cap at the lower of wages or inflation, attached to the property so it survives a change of tenant <Cite k="greens-rent-caps-jul-2026" /> <Tag kind="promise" />; more than 88,000 public homes from a $4 billion initial investment; and stamp duty abolished on owner-occupied homes to $1.1 million, sliding to $1.6 million, paid for by a 5.3% land tax on residential investment holdings above $5 million <Cite k="greens-housing-jul-2026" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. The release says that tax raises $6.4 billion over a decade; the party’s platform page says $2 billion over four years <Cite k="greens-platform-housing" />. Both are Greens figures, and the PBO modelling behind the rent-cap saving they quote wasn’t something I could find. When the Greens tried to introduce a rent-controls bill in April, the motion lost 31 to 48 with Labor voting no; Coalition members voted to allow the debate, which is procedure, not support <Cite k="hansard-rent-controls-apr-2026" />. Nothing in the Greens’ material is specific to regional Victoria.

One Nation matters here because it polled 22% in July <Cite k="pollbludger-resolve-jul-2026" />, not because its housing page is detailed. That page would cut immigration and ban non-citizen ownership, both federal matters; let people use superannuation for a deposit, also federal; and, at state level, review fees and charges, allow tax-free renting of rooms and granny flats, and release land faster <Cite k="onenation-housing" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. It is undated, uncosted and says more is coming. The Victorian Socialists want 15,000 public homes a year and a 25% rent cut followed by a freeze <Cite k="sv-housing" />; I note it because they’re standing in a number of regional seats, not because it’s costed.

Among the regional independents announced so far, Lachlan McKeeman in Benambra names affordable housing as a priority and Ron Nelson in South Barwon is running on infrastructure lagging behind growth. Neither has published a housing policy I could compare, and neither is necessarily on my ballot. That piece waits until nominations close in November.

<h2 id="who-bears-the-cost" tabindex="-1">Who bears the cost, seen from here</h2>

Three things in the comparison look different from a country town than from a Melbourne office.

The first is the short-stay levy. A quarter of its revenue is earmarked for regional housing through Homes Victoria <Cite k="parl-short-stay-levy" />. Repealing it helps the owner of a holiday rental on the coast or in the high country, and removes a regional revenue stream the Coalition hasn’t said how it would replace. Whether the levy has freed up a single long-term rental is a separate question I haven’t seen answered by anyone.

The second is the stamp-duty threshold. Lifting first-home-buyer relief from $750,000 to $1 million is worth up to $55,000 to a buyer in that band <Cite k="wilson-budget-reply-2026" />. The question the Coalition should be asked in a regional seat is how many local first-home buyers are paying between $750,000 and $1 million, because below that the existing relief already applies and the promise changes nothing. I don’t have the figure. I’d like a candidate to.

The third is that renters are the people least served by the two largest parties. Labor’s reforms are real and delivered, and the Tenants Victoria asks for this election are modest: lock them in, extend minimum standards to the roughly third of leases signed before 2021, fund a checker for excessive increases <Cite k="tenants-vic-2026" />. The Coalition has said nothing on renting that I could find. The Greens have said the most and would change the most, with the most uncertainty about what happens to supply in a town where vacancy is already a little over 2%. And the federal money that arrived this month for 234 crisis homes in Geelong, Mildura and Swan Hill <Cite k="abc-haff-sep-2026" /> is a reminder that some of what I want from the state is actually Canberra’s to give.

<h2 id="what-id-need-answered" tabindex="-1">What I’d need answered</h2>

Applying the [five questions](/articles/victoria-election-2026#questions-before-voting). What actually changed: renters’ rights, yes; regional social homes, fewer than 200 new ones against a billion-dollar fund. Who was responsible: the state for planning, tax and public housing; Canberra for rent assistance and the big funds; councils for the schemes they’re now told to rewrite. What supports the claim: Labor’s “completed or underway” and the Coalition’s $534 both need a number behind them. Who bears the cost: for every tax cut on the table, someone in the regions pays for the relief someone else in the regions gets. Is the alternative credible: the Coalition’s tax package is specific and uncosted; its supply plan has no target; the Greens’ plan is specific, costed by the party, and disputed on its own pages.

None of that settles my vote. It does tell me which questions to take to a candidates’ forum, and it tells me that “housing” on a how-to-vote card means something different for a buyer, a renter and someone on the register, and that the parties know it. The [emergency-services levy](/articles/victoria-2026-emergency-services-levy) and [roads](/articles/victoria-2026-roads-emergency-services) comparisons are the earlier parts of this series; energy and climate for regional communities is the next.
