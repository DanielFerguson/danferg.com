<h2 id="from-the-record" tabindex="-1">From the record to the alternatives</h2>

[My first article](/articles/victoria-election-2026) started with the government’s record. I wanted to understand what Labor deserved credit for, which criticisms were supported, and where my own assumptions needed correcting. I’m keeping that version intact so the starting point remains visible.

This article asks the next question: **what would actually change for regional roads and emergency services under the alternatives?**

I’ve traditionally voted Labor, and living in the country has made that vote less comfortable. The roads and the emergency-services levy are practical reasons to look more closely. But being frustrated with the government doesn’t tell me whether someone else’s proposal will work. I need to give the alternatives the same scrutiny.

I’ve compared the published positions I could verify from Labor, the Liberals, the Nationals, the Greens, One Nation and named independents. Some are detailed commitments; others are principles, parliamentary actions or objections. Those differences matter. A party’s silence in the sources I found is a research limit, not proof that it has nothing to say.

<h2 id="starting-point" tabindex="-1">Start with the same problem</h2>

For a particular road, I first need to know who manages it. The state is responsible for its arterial network; councils manage most local roads. Commonwealth money can help fund either without making Canberra the road manager. The [Department of Transport and Planning’s map](https://transport.vic.gov.au/road-and-active-transport/business-and-industry/road-and-traffic-management/map-of-dtp-managed-roads) helps turn a complaint into a question for the right authority.

The September Auditor-General’s report gives the roads concern an independent foundation: **50.38% of regional state roads were rated poor or very poor for remaining useful life in the 2024 survey**. That measures structural life, rather than declaring half the roads immediately unsafe. It also identifies weaknesses in maintenance priorities and oversight. A larger budget needs to address how the work is chosen and checked. [VAGO, 9 September 2026](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads).

For emergency services, I’m checking two things together: what people pay and what responders can deliver. The state sets the levy; councils collect it. The Emergency Services and Volunteers Fund (ESVF) supports a broader set of services than its predecessor, including Victoria State Emergency Service (VICSES) and disaster-response functions, alongside the Country Fire Authority (CFA) and Fire Rescue Victoria (FRV). The [SRO’s explanation](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/owning-property/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund/understanding-emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund) sets out that distinction.

My test is fairly simple. Does a proposal change the funding, the delivery system, or both? Who benefits, who pays, and what would let us tell whether it worked?

<h2 id="roads" tabindex="-1">What would change for roads?</h2>

Labor’s immediate offer is an extra **$352 million** announced on 30 August, with a target of increasing pothole repairs from 250,000 to 500,000 this year. The government identifies savings elsewhere, including **$112 million within the forward estimates** from delaying three level-crossing removals by a year. Deferral changes when money is spent; it doesn’t automatically reduce the eventual project cost. [Government announcement](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/safer-roads-and-fixing-potholes-our-priority).

DTP told the Auditor-General that the addition takes 2026–27 road-asset funding to approximately **$1.39 billion**. That covers more than pavement, including bridges, drains, signs and planning. The announcement was outside the completed audit’s scope, so it hasn’t been validated by the audit as a solution. [VAGO funding explanation](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads).

The Liberals and Nationals’ July offer is **$5 billion over four years**, including preventive maintenance, better drainage and roadside work, a dedicated Better Roads Victoria division within DTP, and reviews of standards and contracts. These are joint commitments, although local Nationals releases sometimes put their own party first. [Joint announcement, 13 July](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/danny-obrien-mp-5-billion-to-eliminate-1-million-potholes-and-get-victorias-roads-back-on-track/).

The institutional part interests me. Rebuilding engineering capability and checking contractors addresses how money becomes a lasting repair. But a new division needs staff, authority and a clear relationship with existing contracts. The release doesn’t supply that implementation plan or independently assessed offsets.

The easy mistake is to divide $5 billion by four, get **$1.25 billion a year**, and declare a winner against Labor’s $1.39 billion. The periods and coverage aren’t aligned. The Coalition originally claimed a 25% increase in July, and Danny O’Brien repeated that comparison on 11 September, after Labor’s additional announcement. I still need both parties’ annual figures on the same basis. [September statement](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/danny-obrien-letter-to-the-editor-12/).

I also won’t treat the Coalition’s one-million-pothole pledge as proof of better roads than 500,000 repairs in one year. Counting repeated patches could reward work that doesn’t last.

There is a clearer distinction on **council roads**. The Coalition’s September pledge adds **$288 million**, providing each of 48 rural and regional councils with **$1.5 million annually for four years**, starting in the **2027–28 Budget**. Councils would select local priorities instead of competing for each grant. The arithmetic is straightforward: 48 × $1.5 million × four = $288 million. [Joint pledge](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/danny-obrien-mp-liberals-and-nationals-to-restore-288-million-country-roads-and-bridges-program/); [commencement date](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/jade-benham-mp-nationals-and-liberals-to-restore-288-million-country-roads-and-bridges-program/).

That predictability has practical value. East Gippsland Shire welcomed it for planning road and bridge works and asked for bipartisan support. Its endorsement doesn’t establish that $1.5 million is enough for every council’s backlog. Equal grants also don’t reflect identical network lengths or repair needs. [Council response, 7 September](https://www.eastgippsland.vic.gov.au/media-releases/country-roads-and-bridge-program-will-support-improvements).

The Greens’ transport policy explicitly includes country-road repair and more freight on rail. It also proposes freight charges related to axle loads and road damage. Their August intervention argues for government capacity to perform preventive maintenance. I’d want to understand the costs and effects on freight operators and farming supply chains, alongside the proposed benefits. Neither document provides a comparable funded maintenance schedule. [Transport policy](https://cdn.greens.org.au/vic/policies/transport-and-freight-policy); [maintenance-capacity position](https://greens.org.au/vic/news/media-release/labor-privatisation-obsession-now-set-push-cost-fixing-victorias-pothole).

One Nation’s current candidate statements include better roads and more durable repairs. Its Victorian policy index marks fiscal management as still being updated. I haven’t located a statewide maintenance allocation, repair timetable or quantified funding source in the material reviewed. That leaves a substantial question before I can compare delivery credibility. [Candidate statements](https://vic.onenation.org.au/candidates); [Victorian policy index](https://vic.onenation.org.au/issues).

<h2 id="regional-share" tabindex="-1">What does a fair regional share mean?</h2>

The Liberals and Nationals also promise **25% of new Victorian Government infrastructure expenditure for regional Victoria**. It is a broader allocation rule, not an extra roads fund to add to the other totals. The Nationals champion this policy, but the announcement explicitly commits a Liberal–National government. [Fair Share Guarantee, 22 May](https://www.jesswilsonkew.com.au/media-releases/2026-05-22-a-fair-share-for-regional-victorians-guaranteed?topic=leader-of-the-liberal-party,treasurer).

This speaks directly to my frustration about uneven investment. It also needs definitions: which projects count, how statewide projects are split, whether maintenance is included, and whether the percentage is measured each year or across a term.

The Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) evidence behind the campaign deserves care. Its 2025–26 analysis counts **estimated lifetime investment in listed projects**, rather than one year’s spending. Regional-only projects represent 12.0% of that total, while 30.4% is statewide, mixed or unspecified. The all-project comparison shows metropolitan investment per person **56.6% higher**. A narrower analysis of projects costing at least $100 million over their lifetime, excluding identified Commonwealth contributions, produces a larger gap. Private and other contributions remain; this isn’t exclusively Victorian Government funding. A claim about “more than double” needs that narrower scope attached. [PBO report, pp.1–5 and 15](https://static.pbo.vic.gov.au/files/PBO_Regional-and-metropolitan-investment-2025-26_PUBLIC.pdf).

I can take regional disadvantage seriously while asking whether the comparison measures it properly. A percentage alone won’t tell me whether the road carrying local freight gets repaired.

<h2 id="levy" tabindex="-1">The levy and its alternatives</h2>

The original proposed farm increase is no longer the current rate. For 2026–27, the farm variable rate remains **28.7 cents per $1,000 of capital improved value**. With an unchanged $1 million valuation and no relief, the current annual totals are **$569 for primary production and $312 for residential land**. These are illustrative properties, not my bill or typical households. Classification matters more than a simple country-versus-city distinction. [Current SRO rates](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund-current-rates).

Eligible volunteers can obtain rebates, with a **$3,152 farm cap for 2026–27**, subject to ownership, service and application conditions. Neither volunteering somewhere in the community nor owning a rural property automatically qualifies. [Volunteer guidelines](https://service.vic.gov.au/find-services/housing-and-property/eligible-volunteers-rebate-scheme/guideline).

Labor’s case is that the broader levy provides reliable funding for more emergency services. The budget forecasts **$1.6 billion in receipts for 2026–27**. That is gross revenue, not $1.6 billion of entirely new services. Some services and their funding existed before the reform. [Budget Paper 5, p.177](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/2026-27-statement-finances).

This is the distinction I want explained publicly. Directing levy receipts to emergency services can still free up general revenue that previously paid for those services. That accounting change doesn’t establish unlawful diversion, and it doesn’t establish that every additional levy dollar purchases extra capacity. I need an agency-by-agency comparison of the previous budget, new funding and actual delivery. [Treasurer’s explanation in the May 2025 debate](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/parliamentary-activity/hansard/hansard-details/HANSARD-974425065-30856).

The Coalition’s proposed reversal would restore the earlier property-levy model and fund SES and other services through the state budget. **It is not a promise to remove every property-based fire-service charge.** The first-100-days plan seeks a parliamentary sitting before Christmas to pass repeal legislation. The eventual bill, parliamentary support, rates and commencement provisions still matter. [Replacement model stated in Parliament](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/parliamentary-activity/hansard/hansard-details/HANSARD-2145855009-30868); [first-100-days plan](https://vicliberal.org.au/media-releases/2026-08-19-liberals-and-nationals-launch-our-first-100-days-plan).

I understand the appeal of reducing a rates-notice burden. But shifting costs to the general budget still requires revenue, savings or borrowing. I haven’t located a public, independently assessed reconciliation of the replacement levy, promised service funding and offsets. Nor would I assume old rates simply return unchanged or that current volunteer rebates automatically survive.

The Nationals’ current policy page proposes pausing and reviewing the Suburban Rail Loop; the joint economic plan proposes freezing recruitment in parts of the public service. Those are proposed ways to free up money, but a long-term saving isn’t necessarily available when a new commitment starts. I want the net savings, timing and service effects connected to these promises. Rebuilding DTP’s engineering capability would also require clarity about which recruitment remains possible. [Current Nationals policies](https://vic.nationals.org.au/policies/); [joint economic plan](https://vic.nationals.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/VicNat_2026-10-Year-Plan_Booklet_SD_v1.9.pdf).

The Greens supported the amended scheme after negotiating changes including **$10 million annually for FRV fleet replacement** and farmer relief. The legislation sets the levy-funded share of annual funding requirements at **90% for FRV and 95% for CFA and VICSES**. Those percentages do **not** freeze the size of the agencies’ overall dollar budgets. [Greens’ account](https://greens.org.au/vic/news/media-release/greens-secure-significant-changes-make-levy-fairer-exemptions-farmers-and); [current Act, s12(2B)–(2C)](https://content.legislation.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-07/12-58aa035-authorised.pdf). The Treasurer acknowledged that distinction in the [parliamentary debate](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/parliamentary-activity/hansard/hansard-details/HANSARD-974425065-30856).

One Nation’s [opposition to the levy](https://vic.onenation.org.au/farmer-fines-scrap) is clear. Its Northern Victoria MP, Rikkie-Lee Tyrrell, voted against the bill’s second reading, and its announced Wendouree candidate Stuart Robinson campaigns to scrap the tax. The current sources don’t give me a replacement rate schedule or quantified funding arrangement. I shouldn’t quietly substitute the Coalition’s old-levy proposal for One Nation’s position. [Division record](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/parliamentary-activity/hansard/hansard-details/HANSARD-974425065-30856); [current candidate statements](https://vic.onenation.org.au/candidates).

<h2 id="response-capacity" tabindex="-1">What reaches the responders?</h2>

The services themselves need more attention than their funding label. The May budget announced **$100 million for CFA tankers and pumpers**, **$26.2 million for stations, land and related work**, and **$22.4 million for volunteer support, training and protective equipment**. VICSES has **$17.4 million for facilities and land**, plus **$3.6 million to bring two new units into operation**. These are commitment totals, not necessarily expenditure in 2026–27; the release doesn’t establish that every vehicle or facility has been delivered. [May budget announcement, p.4](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/2026-27-State-Budget-media-release-Labor-Delivers-What-We-Need-To-Keep-Victorians-Safe.pdf).

The equipment grants programme also shows why community effort remains part of the picture: the announced **$31 million 2026–27 round** offers $2 from government for each $1 raised locally. That can help buy equipment while still requiring fundraising. I want to know whether communities have the people and capacity to make use of it. [VESEP announcement](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/record-31-million-boost-emergency-services).

The Coalition has also made direct commitments. On 29 July, Danny O’Brien promised a fixed, transparent fleet-replacement schedule under a Liberal–National government. On 1 September, the parties promised a new three-bay station for Hazelwood North CFA and repeated the fleet pledge. Those releases don’t specify a statewide fleet budget, replacement dates, or the station’s cost and completion date. [July commitment](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/danny-obrien-mp-bushfire-inquiry-confirms-labor-left-cfa-exposed-and-ill-equipped/); [Hazelwood North commitment](https://vic.nationals.org.au/media-releases/martin-cameron-mp-nationals-to-deliver-new-station-for-hazelwood-north-cfa/).

The Liberals and Nationals’ July bushfire-inquiry minority report also recommends indexed CFA funding, a costed ten-year fleet plan and removal of frontline single-cab tankers within five years. Those are recommendations by the minority members, not findings adopted by the whole committee or automatically funded election promises. [Minority report](https://www.melinabath.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Inquiry-into-the-2026-Bushfires-Nationals-and-Liberals-Minority-Report.pdf).

<h2 id="independents" tabindex="-1">Where independents fit</h2>

There is no single independent platform. I need to identify the person, the electorate and the particular change they would seek.

Dr Lachlan McKeeman is an announced community-backed independent candidate for **Benambra**, in north-east Victoria. I could verify his campaign, but the home and about pages reviewed don’t specify a road-maintenance programme or whether he would retain, change or replace the levy. His CFA involvement doesn’t tell me how he would vote on it. Those are questions to resolve, rather than policies I can fill in for him. [Campaign](https://drlachiemckeeman.com.au/); [about McKeeman](https://drlachiemckeeman.com.au/about).

There is a concrete parliamentary example elsewhere: **Adem Somyurek, the current independent member for Northern Metropolitan**, opposed the levy bill’s second reading and supported referring it to an inquiry in May 2025. That records an action, not a costed alternative. His 2026 recontest plans weren’t verified in this research. [Parliamentary profile](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/members/adem-somyurek/); [division record](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/parliamentary-activity/hansard/hansard-details/HANSARD-974425065-30856).

These examples aren’t interchangeable choices on one ballot. Victoria elects district and regional representatives, and formal nominations don’t open until November. The [VEC’s election page](https://www.vec.vic.gov.au/voting/2026-state-election) is the reference for checking the actual choices where you live. I’ll assess named independents on their proposals and record as those become clearer.

<h2 id="credible-promises" tabindex="-1">What would make a promise credible?</h2>

I don’t expect an opposition to have delivered a policy it hasn’t had the power to implement. I do expect enough detail to assess the promise. The government has an additional responsibility: explaining what its existing money and authority have achieved.

A useful costing shows the years covered, existing funding, new money, offsets and assumptions. It should also show what happens when proposals interact. Removing a revenue source while promising better services creates a question for the whole package, even when each announcement sounds reasonable on its own.

The Victorian Parliamentary Budget Office explains that its costings include methodology and uncertainty, and remain confidential unless the client requests publication. So I’ll distinguish **a costing I can inspect** from **one I haven’t located publicly**. I can’t infer that work was never done. [PBO operational plan, p.15](https://static.pbo.vic.gov.au/files/PBO_Operational_Plan_2026-27.pdf).

For roads, I want published priorities, the balance between patching and renewal, quality checks and evidence about repeat failures. For emergency services, I want fleet delivery dates, useful equipment, training and available crews. A nominal increase can still purchase less if costs rise; a new vehicle does little for response capacity without people who can use it.

These are the questions I’d bring to a candidate or a conversation with a friend:

1. **What changes from the current rules or budget?** Name the road owner, service or property class involved.
2. **What is additional, and who pays?** Show the funding period and what is cut, deferred or raised elsewhere.
3. **What would reach our community, and when?** Identify eligibility, local discretion and delivery dependencies.
4. **How will we judge the result?** Agree on measures beyond money announced or a photograph at a handover.
5. **What evidence would change our minds?** Leave room for a corrected figure, a better proposal or disappointing delivery.

<h2 id="where-that-leaves-me" tabindex="-1">Where that leaves my vote</h2>

This comparison gives me something more useful than a list of reasons to be annoyed. Labor needs to demonstrate that its funding and reforms produce dependable services. The Liberals and Nationals need to show the full budget behind their roads package and levy reversal. The Greens’ public-maintenance proposal needs a budget and timetable, while their levy agreement needs to be assessed against what reaches responders. One Nation’s opposition to the levy needs an equally clear account of what replaces it. Independents need to identify the changes they would try to secure.

Those are different questions because the published positions and records are different. Applying a consistent standard doesn’t require pretending the evidence is equally complete.

I still want reliable regional roads, emergency services that can respond, and a fair explanation of who pays. My vote will involve weighing those priorities alongside other services and the broader budget. Health, housing and energy need their own comparisons; I’m leaving them for the next pieces.

I haven’t settled my vote. I want the next round of promises to make those choices clearer: which roads get lasting repairs, what reaches responders, and who pays.
