Every article in this series so far has ended the same way: a promise with no number attached to it. Five billion for roads with no annual profile. A stamp-duty cut with no published cost. Firming tenders with no megawatts and no price. Eighty-eight thousand public homes funded by a tax the party costs two different ways on two different pages. This is the article where I stop asking what each promise does and start asking what the budget can carry, because that question sits underneath all of them.

I should say plainly that I find this the hardest part of [the guide](/articles/victoria-election-2026) to be honest about. Debt is the topic where my instincts are least reliable: the figures are big enough to mean nothing, the political framing is loudest, and I have no professional grounding to fall back on. So this piece leans harder on the Auditor-General than on anyone's press release.

<h2 id="the-same-problem" tabindex="-1">Start with the same problem</h2>

The May budget's headline is an operating surplus: about $700 million in 2025–26 and $1 billion in 2026–27, rising to $2 billion by 2029–30, the first in seven years and the only one on the eastern seaboard <Cite k={["budget-fiscal-strategy", "abc-budget-2026-wrap"]} /> <Tag kind="govt" />. That is real on the measure it uses. On the cash measure, the same budget papers carry cash deficits of more than $30 billion across the four years <Cite k="aap-budget-2026-debt" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />. The economist Saul Eslake put the consequence in one line: until you start running cash surpluses, you can't begin to repay debt.

Both of those are true at once, and which one a politician reaches for tells you what they're arguing. An operating surplus says day-to-day spending is covered by day-to-day revenue. A cash deficit says the state is still borrowing, mostly to build things. Net debt is $175.6 billion in 2026–27, about 24.9% of the state's economy, heading to $199.3 billion by mid-2030 <Cite k="aap-budget-2026-debt" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />.

The number that changed how I think about this is the interest. Victoria is paying about $24.4 million a day now, and on the budget's own projections $32.3 million a day, or more than $11 billion a year, by 2029–30 <Cite k="abc-budget-2026-wrap" />. The Auditor-General puts last year's interest at $6.8 billion, which was 6.7% of all revenue, forecast to reach $10.6 billion by 2028–29 <Cite k="vago-afr-2024-25" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. Set that beside the road maintenance figure from [part two](/articles/victoria-2026-regional-roads): the department's own business case says holding pavement condition where it is would cost more than $3 billion a year, and about $1.39 billion is allocated <Cite k="vago-roads" />. The interest bill is roughly three times the gap that is letting regional roads fall apart <Tag kind="author" />. Interest is not a villain — it's the price of a decade of building — but it is the largest single competitor to every promise in this series.

The rest of the Auditor-General's November report is the most useful thing I read for this article, partly because it refuses to be a headline. The 2024–25 accounts got an unqualified opinion: the books are accurate. They also showed a net operating loss of $2.6 billion, accumulated losses of $50.6 billion over six years, gross debt up from $168.8 billion to $187.9 billion and heading for $236.6 billion by 2029, the highest debt-to-economy ratio of any state at around 30%, and cash deficits every single year since 2016–17. Its conclusion is that debt growth "has consistently outpaced" revenue and economic growth <Cite k="vago-afr-2024-25" /> <Tag kind="independent" />.

What nobody I could find says is that this is a solvency crisis. S&P still has Victoria at AA and Moody's at Aa2, both stable <Cite k="dtf-credit-rating" />, though S&P's analyst told the ABC on budget day that the risk is it becomes unsustainable and that there is "no, at the moment, path to repayment" <Cite k="abc-budget-2026-wrap" />. The disagreement among people who do this for a living is not about whether the state can pay its bills. It's about the path back and what the interest crowds out on the way.

<h2 id="labors-position" tabindex="-1">What Labor says, and what it does</h2>

The government's own account is a five-step strategy, and it says it is three steps in: growth, then an operating cash surplus, then operating surpluses, then stabilising net debt as a share of the economy, then reducing it <Cite k="budget-fiscal-strategy" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. Steps four and five are in the forecasts rather than the record. Revenue is doing the work: land tax at $7.6 billion, payroll tax about $12.5 billion, land transfer duty at $10 billion and forecast to reach $12.4 billion by 2029–30, with total tax collections passing $50 billion a year by 2030 <Cite k="pitcher-budget-2026" />. That growth is mostly the tax system meeting rising values and wages, not a decision anyone has had to defend at an election.

Ben Carroll's six weeks have produced two fiscal decisions, and they point in opposite directions. He abolished the undisclosed 1% rail charge that had been added to public transport fares at the start of 2025 and never disclosed in a budget paper or a media release — a charge the Auditor-General found was projected to raise about $8 billion to 2062 and was to be the Suburban Rail Loop's largest source of value-capture revenue <Cite k={["vago-srl-2026", "abc-srl"]} /> <Tag kind="independent" />. Abolishing it was right, and the reason he gave — that there was no transparency in its creation — is the correct reason.

In the same week he claimed $1 billion in savings on the Suburban Rail Loop by cutting two underground interchanges and a car park. The Auditor-General's finding is that those items were never in the project's cost estimate in the first place <Cite k="abc-srl" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. You cannot save money you had not budgeted. That is a small thing against $33 billion, and it is exactly the kind of claim this guide exists to catch.

Labor's attack on the alternative has the same problem in reverse. A release from last October puts a $10.8 billion figure on five Coalition commitments and says the consequence would be sacking up to 4,500 police, 9,000 teachers and 18,000 nurses <Cite k="premier-10-8-blackhole" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. It names the five commitments, which is more than the Coalition has done, and it shows no arithmetic at all. I can't use a number I can't follow, from either side.

<h2 id="the-coalition" tabindex="-1">The Coalition's plan, and what's missing from it</h2>

The Coalition's fiscal frame is the clearest of anyone's: a cash surplus by 2032, a payroll tax threshold lifted to $1.2 million by 2028–29 and the metropolitan rate cut to 4.8% from 2030–31, a Charter of Budget Honesty with a real-time expenditure tracker, and six taxes repealed or reduced <Cite k="lib-10-year-plan" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. A cash surplus is the right target, and going for the cash measure rather than the accrual one is the more demanding promise of the two. The Charter, after the fare-levy business, is hard to argue with.

Then there is the list. Across this series I have recorded the following Coalition commitments with no published cost: abolishing the emergency-services levy, which raises about $1.6 billion gross in 2026–27; first-home-buyer stamp duty relief to $1 million; restoring the land-tax threshold to $300,000; repealing the short-stay levy; the payroll tax changes; abolishing the "schools tax" and the "GP tax"; $5 billion for roads over four years; $288 million for council roads and bridges; 3,000 extra police; and gas-firmed generation tenders of unstated size. Against that sits a set of offsets: scrapping Treaty, which the party costs at $1 billion; a 2% smaller public sector through attrition; a back-office hiring freeze; pausing the Suburban Rail Loop; cancelling VNI West; and a royal commission into the Big Build <Cite k={["lib-10-year-plan", "wilson-budget-reply-2026"]} />.

Three things about that pairing. No offset is attached to any particular commitment, so there is no way to check whether the sums meet. The $15 billion of "Big Build corruption" the royal commission is meant to end traces back to an estimate the CFMEU administrator himself redacted as untested <Cite k="watson-review" />, so it is not a saving anyone can bank. And the 2% public sector reduction has no dollar figure attached that I could find, which means the largest recurring saving in the plan is also the least specified.

To be fair about the gap: James Newbury promised in May last year that every announcement would be fully costed and released before the election, and the Parliamentary Budget Office's election commitment tracker is a page my tools can't read, so I can't tell whether costings have been lodged. "No published costing that I could find" is not "never costed", and the PBO keeps client costings confidential unless a party asks for publication. What I can say is that with ten weeks to go, I could not find them, and the party that is promising a Charter of Budget Honesty is the one with the most unpriced list.

<h2 id="who-bears-the-cost" tabindex="-1">Who bears the cost, seen from here</h2>

This is where I have to correct myself, and it's the most useful thing in the article.

My standing grievance is that Melbourne gets the enormous projects while the country waits. The Parliamentary Budget Office looked at exactly that last November, at Danny O'Brien's request, and found asset investment of $19,338 a head in metropolitan Victoria against $12,348 in the regions — 56.6% more per person <Cite k="pbo-regional-metro" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. That is the figure the Coalition quotes, and it's accurate.

The same document says that if you exclude five major projects, metropolitan investment per person falls to $9,632, which is 22% *below* the regional figure. Regional Victoria has 48.4% of the state's projects and 12% of the investment <Cite k="pbo-regional-metro" />. So the ordinary program — schools, hospitals, roads, the things a town actually notices — runs slightly in the country's favour per head, and the whole of the gap is the megaprojects. My grievance survives, but it is specifically a grievance about the Suburban Rail Loop and its siblings, not about the state neglecting country schools. That is a narrower and more defensible thing to be angry about, and it's also the one the interest bill comes from <Tag kind="author" />.

The part that doesn't get corrected is who pays for the borrowing. Land tax, payroll tax and stamp duty fall wherever the property and the payroll are; the emergency-services levy falls on land, which in the country means working farms ([part three](/articles/victoria-2026-emergency-services-levy)); and the interest is paid out of a revenue pool that would otherwise cover road maintenance and regional health. When a country voter asks what the Suburban Rail Loop has to do with them, the honest answer isn't "nothing". It's the interest.

What the councils out here are asking for is modest by comparison: Rural Councils Victoria wants a $1 billion rural and regional productivity fund with at least a quarter guaranteed for rural councils, separate state-road maintenance money, and the Safe Local Roads and Streets Program extended past 2027 <Cite k="rcv-election-platform" />. The 2026–27 budget's regional lines are smaller and more scattered than that: $481 million for regional transport services, $318 million for regional rail, $277 million for regional education and health, $75 million for the Regional Health Infrastructure Fund, $84 million for agriculture, $73 million for road upgrades <Cite k="budget-regional-2026" /> <Tag kind="funded" />. Neither major party has adopted the councils' ask. The Coalition's Fair Share Guarantee answers the first half of the PBO finding and says nothing about the second.

<h2 id="what-id-need-answered" tabindex="-1">What I'd need answered</h2>

Applying the [five questions](/articles/victoria-election-2026#questions-before-voting). What actually changed: an operating surplus arrived and the cash deficit didn't go away; debt and interest both rose; one hidden charge was abolished and one savings claim was overstated. Who was responsible: twelve years of decisions by three premiers, a Commonwealth that sets the GST and put $6 billion into the Loop, and councils that collect a state levy. What supports the claim: the Auditor-General, on nearly everything here, because both parties' own numbers come with holes in them. Who bears the cost: the interest is paid by everyone and spends the money that would have fixed the roads. Is the alternative credible: it has the more demanding target, the clearer institutional fix and the longest list of unpriced promises, all at once.

I came into this half-expecting to find that the debt talk was overdone, and half-expecting a catastrophe. It's neither. Nobody with a rating agency or an audit mandate thinks Victoria can't pay; everybody with one thinks the interest is eating into what the state can do, and no party has shown me a credible path back to cash surplus with its own promises included. That is the thing I'd put to any candidate, in any seat: not "how big is the debt", but "which of your promises comes second".
