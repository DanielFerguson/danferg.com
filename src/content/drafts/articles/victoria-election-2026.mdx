<h2 id="why-im-looking" tabindex="-1">Why I’m looking more closely this time</h2>

I’ve traditionally voted Labor. That’s worth putting on the table before I start assessing a Labor government.

Living in the country has made that vote less comfortable. I’m frustrated by the state of our roads, by potholes, and by the feeling that regional communities sit a long way down the list while Melbourne gets another enormous infrastructure project. The proposed emergency-services levy added to that frustration. Asking more of communities that already volunteer and fundraise deserves a proper explanation.

Politics is also turning up in more conversations with friends, family and neighbours, and in more of what I read online. I wanted something more useful to bring to those conversations than whatever headline had annoyed me most recently.

So I’ve started checking. What has actually changed? Which criticisms survive a look at the evidence? Where have I accepted a claim because it suits the way I already vote?

This first version looks at the government’s record and the questions I want answered before deciding. It’s a reference I can send to someone, including someone who thinks I’ve got it wrong. I expect parts of it to change as I learn more.

<h2 id="what-im-checking" tabindex="-1">What I’m checking</h2>

Victoria votes on **28 November 2026**. The [Victorian Electoral Commission’s election page](https://www.vec.vic.gov.au/voting/2026-state-election) is the place to check enrolment, candidates and voting arrangements.

Responsibility matters. State governments run public hospitals and schools and make many transport, housing and emergency-service decisions. Canberra controls things such as Medicare and income tax, while funding also crosses government boundaries. Councils have their own responsibilities. The [Parliamentary Education Office’s explanation](https://peo.gov.au/understand-our-parliament/how-parliament-works/three-levels-of-government/three-levels-of-government) is a useful starting point.

For roads, the state generally manages major arterial roads and freeways; councils manage most local roads. Some roads have other managers. Before blaming a premier for a particular pothole, I can check the [Department of Transport and Planning’s road map](https://transport.vic.gov.au/road-and-active-transport/business-and-industry/road-and-traffic-management/map-of-dtp-managed-roads).

I’m also separating four things that political arguments often blur: **a promise, money spent, something delivered, and an outcome demonstrated**. A hospital opening is real. Whether people receive timely care needs another measure. A funding announcement tells me less than completed repairs that last.

Government sources help establish what operates. Independent watchdogs help assess performance and conduct. Party documents establish what a party proposes. None answers every question on its own.

<h2 id="the-years-behind-this-election" tabindex="-1">The years behind this election</h2>

Labor’s record stretches across Daniel Andrews, Jacinta Allan and now Ben Carroll. Carroll became Premier on **28 July 2026**; he has inherited nearly twelve years of government, with only weeks in the top job so far. That calls for some care about personal credit and responsibility. [Victorian Government: the Premier](https://www.vic.gov.au/premier).

<h2 id="what-deserves-credit" tabindex="-1">What the government deserves credit for</h2>

There are substantial achievements here. Acknowledging them doesn’t commit me to another Labor vote, but leaving them out would make this a poor assessment.

### Transport people can use

The **Metro Tunnel opened in November 2025**, with five new stations and its full timetable change following in February 2026. By September, **94 level crossings had been removed**. These are completed changes to the network, although an opening alone doesn’t settle arguments about costs or value. [Metro Tunnel overview](https://bigbuild.vic.gov.au/projects/metro-tunnel/about/overview); [Melton opening and crossing count](https://bigbuild.vic.gov.au/news/level-crossing-removal-project/new-melton-station-opens-with-94-level-crossings-gone-for-good).

The regional fare cap is more directly relevant outside Melbourne: since March 2023, regional daily fares have been capped at the metropolitan daily maximum. Under-18s gained free travel using a Youth myki from January 2026. Cheaper fares matter, alongside whether a useful service reaches your town. [Public Transport Ombudsman](https://www.ptovic.com.au/news-publications/news/the-daily-travel-cap-in-regional-victoria-now-matches-fares-in-metro-melbourne); [Transport Victoria’s eligibility rules](https://transport.vic.gov.au/news-and-resources/news/free-travel-for-children-and-eligible-concession-travellers).

### Education and health

**Free TAFE began in 2019**, before the later federal program. The government reported more than 245,000 students benefiting by April 2026. **Free Kinder began in 2023**, covering funded programs at participating services, rather than making all childcare free. These benefits reach regional families too. [TAFE history](https://www.vic.gov.au/seven-years-free-tafe-victoria); [TAFE participation](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/free-tafe-bigger-better-guaranteed); [Kinder launch](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/more-money-families-pockets-free-kinder-kicks); [current Kinder rules](https://www.vic.gov.au/free-kinder).

The school building authority reports **121 new schools opened between 2017 and 2026**. That’s a government delivery figure; educational outcomes require separate assessment. [School building authority](https://www.schoolbuildings.vic.gov.au/about-us).

New health facilities include the **Victorian Heart Hospital**, opened in 2023, and the **new Footscray Hospital**, opened in February 2026. Bendigo’s hospital opened in 2017, with its second stage finished in 2018. Its construction began before Andrews took office, so Labor shares credit with its predecessor and the people who delivered it. [Heart Hospital](https://www.vhba.vic.gov.au/health/specialist-centres/victorian-heart-hospital); [Footscray opening](https://www.vhba.vic.gov.au/news/new-footscray-hospital-opens-its-doors-patients); [Bendigo project history](https://www.vhba.vic.gov.au/health/hospitals/bendigo-hospital).

Labor also legislated nurse-to-patient ratios in 2015, protecting arrangements with an earlier history, and introduced public fertility care in 2022. Staffing, waiting times and regional access still need scrutiny alongside those reforms. [Ratios legislation](https://www.health.vic.gov.au/nursing-and-midwifery/nursing-and-midwifery-legislation-and-regulation); [public fertility care](https://www.health.vic.gov.au/public-health/public-fertility-care).

### Household support and social reform

Rental reforms have strengthened tenants’ rights, including the November 2025 removal of the remaining no-fault end-of-fixed-term eviction ground. Solar Homes has supported household installations; renewables supplied **42.4% of Victorian electricity generation in 2024–25**. That’s electricity generation, not all energy use, and the result involves private investment, households and federal policy as well as Victoria. [Rental changes](https://www.consumer.vic.gov.au/housing/renting/new-changes-to-the-rental-laws); [Solar Homes delivery](https://www.solar.vic.gov.au/solar-victoria-hits-500000-approvals); [renewable energy reporting](https://www.energy.vic.gov.au/renewable-energy/victorian-renewable-energy-and-storage-targets).

I also give weight to voluntary assisted dying, operating since 2019 following a conscience vote; the ban on harmful conversion practices; and the family violence reforms following the Royal Commission. The government reported implementing all 227 recommendations in 2023. Completing recommendations doesn’t mean family violence has been solved. [Assisted dying](https://www.health.vic.gov.au/voluntary-assisted-dying/about); [conversion practices](https://www.humanrights.vic.gov.au/change-or-suppression-practices/reporting-practices/); [Royal Commission implementation](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/landmark-royal-commission-recommendations-implemented).

Victoria’s statewide Treaty commenced in December 2025. Credit belongs to First Peoples’ representatives and communities as well as the government. Its practical outcomes will need to be judged over time. [Treaty milestones](https://www.firstpeoplesrelations.vic.gov.au/treaty-negotiation-updates).

<h2 id="regional-roads" tabindex="-1">Why regional roads deserve serious scrutiny</h2>

This is where my frustration has a strong independent foundation.

The Auditor-General’s **9 September 2026** report found an increasing share of state roads rated poor or very poor for remaining useful life. The regional figure was **50.38% in 2024**. [Maintaining State Roads](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads).

<VictoriaRoadsComparison />

That measure estimates structural life: “poor” means five to fifteen years remaining, and “very poor” means under five. It is evidence of mounting maintenance needs, not a declaration that every road in those categories is currently unsafe.

The audit records DTP’s advice that funding was insufficient and maintenance increasingly reactive, alongside gaps in prioritisation and contractor oversight. The gap between defect job records and repairs more than doubled from January 2025 to June 2026. But pavement maintenance spending rose from **$466.5 million in 2022–23 to $611.7 million in 2024–25**. The criticism is about adequacy and results; a simple story of spending continually falling would be wrong. [VAGO’s findings and measures](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads).

Carroll announced an additional **$352 million** on 30 August, taking the stated 2026–27 road asset management allocation to about **$1.39 billion**. This came after the audit work concluded. The government’s proposed budget offsets include delaying three crossing removals by a year. I want to see which roads improve and whether repairs last, beyond a larger pothole tally. [Government announcement](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/safer-roads-and-fixing-potholes-our-priority); [VAGO’s funding reconciliation](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/maintaining-state-roads).

I also need to correct the temptation to connect every road tragedy to a pothole. TAC records 295 deaths in 2023, 284 in 2024 and 291 in 2025. Those totals neither establish exponentially increasing deaths nor identify potholes as the cause. My concern about safety is real; the causal claim needs evidence I don’t have. [TAC annual road deaths](https://www.tac.vic.gov.au/road-safety/statistics/lives-lost-annual).

<h2 id="emergency-services-levy" tabindex="-1">What happened to the emergency-services levy</h2>

This issue made me angry, and checking the detail has changed the way I would describe it.

The **Emergency Services and Volunteers Fund levy replaced the Fire Services Property Levy on 1 July 2025**, broadening the services funded. Councils collect it, but the state sets the scheme. It combines a fixed charge with a variable charge based on **capital improved value**: land plus buildings and improvements. Land-use classification matters; a country address alone doesn’t make a property primary production. [SRO explanation](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/owning-property/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund/understanding-emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund); [calculations and classifications](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/owning-property/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund-calculations-and-discounts).

The original proposed farm variable rate was **83 cents per $1,000 of value**, up from 28.7 cents. On 15 May 2025, the government reduced the proposal to **71.8 cents**, before announcing a freeze at **28.7 cents** on 30 May for 2025–26 as drought relief. December’s Budget Update extended the relief, and the current 2026–27 schedule retains that rate. Those successive versions explain why people can recall very different increases. [May amendment](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-05/250515-Equipping-Our-Emergency-Services-For-The-Future.pdf); [drought freeze](https://www.premier.vic.gov.au/expanding-drought-support-farmers-across-our-state); [Budget Update, pp.132–133](https://www.dtf.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-12/2025-26-Budget-Update.pdf); [current SRO rates](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund-current-rates).

Here’s a deliberately simple comparison using the [historical rates](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/historical-rates/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund-and-fire-services-property-levy-historical-rates) and [2026–27 rates](https://www.sro.vic.gov.au/about-us/rates-and-statistics/current-rates/emergency-services-and-volunteers-fund-current-rates):

<VictoriaLevyExample />

The residential calculation is **$132 + $87 = $219**, becoming **$139 + $173 = $312**. Primary production is **$267 + $287 = $554**, becoming **$282 + $287 = $569**.

The farm example still pays about **82% more** than the equally valued residential property in 2026–27. But that is a comparison between land uses in one year. It is different from the farm’s **2.7% increase across these two years**, and different again from the original proposed farm rate. I shouldn’t turn it into a claim that every country household now pays 100–200% more than a Melbourne household.

Eligible CFA, VICSES and Shepparton Search and Rescue volunteers can seek rebates for a principal residence or qualifying farm. The farm rebate cap rises to **$3,152 in 2026–27**. Eligibility includes service requirements and an annual application; community fundraising alone doesn’t establish entitlement. Applications can be made before or after paying the rates notice. [Current volunteer rebate guidelines](https://service.vic.gov.au/find-services/housing-and-property/eligible-volunteers-rebate-scheme/guideline).

Freezing a variable rate doesn’t freeze every bill: fixed charges and valuations can change. Nor does temporary relief settle the future design. My remaining question is whether property value is a fair way to distribute the cost, particularly where a valuable working property produces a modest income. Any alternative still needs to explain how reliable emergency services will be funded.

<h2 id="integrity-findings" tabindex="-1">What the integrity investigations actually found</h2>

Some criticisms are supported by serious findings. I don’t need to exaggerate them to take them seriously. An opposition allegation starts a question; an investigation may substantiate misconduct, identify corruption risks or make a corruption finding. A criminal conviction is a separate matter.

**Operation Watts, 2022.** The joint IBAC–Ombudsman investigation found widespread misuse of public resources for factional purposes, including publicly funded staff doing party work, alongside nepotism and bullying. It examined conduct involving former ministers Adem Somyurek and Marlene Kairouz. Investigators could not establish a criminal offence under the applicable law, and did not establish that community grants paid party membership fees. Those limits don’t make the substantiated conduct acceptable. [Full joint report](https://www.ombudsman.vic.gov.au/our-impact/investigation-reports/operation-watts-a-joint-investigation-into-allegations-of-serious-corrupt-conduct-involving-victorian-public-officers-including-members-of-parliament/).

**Operation Daintree, 2023.** IBAC examined a $1.2 million health training contract awarded to a Health Workers Union-linked entity. It found improper political influence and failures of public duty around procurement and administration, and criticised oversight. It did **not** make a corrupt-conduct finding. [IBAC’s account, annual report p.39](https://www.parliament.vic.gov.au/globalassets/tabled-paper-documents/tabled-paper-7598/ibac-annual-report-2022_23.pdf).

**Operation Richmond, August 2026.** A central episode concerned negotiations with the United Firefighters Union in **2016**. IBAC found Andrews and senior figures bypassed proper processes, excluding responsible decision-makers and giving union secretary Peter Marshall extraordinary influence. It made **no corrupt-conduct finding**, and the blackmail allegation that prompted the investigation was **not substantiated**. The conduct and the report belong to different premierships. [Richmond findings](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-08-05/vic-operation-richmond-ibac-report-daniel-andrews-ufu/106997618).

**The Commonwealth Games.** The Auditor-General found the abandoned event cost more than **$589 million**, including a **$380 million settlement**. The original business case understated costs and overstated benefits; agencies failed to give adequate, timely advice. The government’s later public estimate of the cost of holding the Games was also overstated. This is a documented failure of planning and public administration, without needing to call it a criminal corruption finding. [March 2024 Games audit](https://www.audit.vic.gov.au/report/withdrawal-2026-commonwealth-games?section=34600--what-we-found).

**Pandemic governance.** The hotel quarantine inquiry documented serious failures of responsibility and oversight. Separately, the Ombudsman found the immediate public housing tower lockdown breached human rights, while accepting that a temporary lockdown was warranted. The government disputed the legal conclusion. Acknowledging the difficulty of the pandemic cannot erase those findings. [Hotel quarantine final report](https://quarantineinquiry.archive.royalcommission.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/0387_rc_covid-19-final-report_volume-1_2_digital.pdf); [tower lockdown investigation](https://www.ombudsman.vic.gov.au/our-impact/news/public-housing-tower-lockdown/).

**The public service.** In 2023, the Ombudsman identified creeping politicisation and damaging secrecy, while not substantiating the specific allegation of improper stacking with ALP operatives. Again, both parts belong in an honest account. [Public service investigation](https://www.ombudsman.vic.gov.au/our-impact/news/creeping-politicisation-of-public-sector-a-reality-in-victoria-ombudsman/).

Two other matters need careful attribution. **Operation Sandon** exposed serious corruption involving Casey councillors and a developer, alongside weaknesses in planning and lobbying controls. It was not a finding that a Victorian premier was corrupt. [IBAC’s Sandon findings](https://newshub.medianet.com.au/2023/07/ibacs-operation-sandon-uncovers-serious-corruption-risks-in-victorias-planning-system/14262/).

The **2026 Geoffrey Watson review of the Victorian CFMEU**, commissioned by the union’s administrator, documented corruption, intimidation and government inaction. It deserves attention. It is not an IBAC finding or a criminal judgment against a premier. The review does not establish an audited total loss to taxpayers. [Watson’s review](https://www.cfmeuinquiry.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/897476/gw-13-watson-report-victoria-redacted.pdf).

Taken together, these findings give me substantial reasons to question how power has been exercised. A new leader needs to show what changes in practice, including the independence of advice, procurement and scrutiny.

<h2 id="information-and-influence" tabindex="-1">How information shapes the conversation</h2>

I came into this concerned about Murdoch’s influence and the amount of political material moving through social media. That concern also needs checking. Otherwise I’m applying a standard to other people’s assumptions that I won’t apply to my own.

Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky’s **Manufacturing Consent**, first published in 1988, offers a framework for thinking about how ownership, advertising, sourcing and institutional incentives shape news. It helps me ask what gets attention, whose account is treated as authoritative and what gets left out. It does not establish that a particular Victorian story is false. [Publisher’s account of the book](https://www.penguin.com.au/books/manufacturing-consent-9781407054056).

Media ownership and commercial incentives deserve scrutiny. News Corp’s portfolio includes the **Herald Sun** and **The Weekly Times**, reaching into both metropolitan and rural news. That makes its editorial choices relevant to this conversation, without making ownership a verdict on every story. [News Corp’s own brand directory](https://www.newscorpaustralia.com/growth-solutions/audience-networks/).

The platforms and creators distributing news matter too. The University of Canberra’s **Digital News Report Australia 2026** found social media had overtaken direct visits to news websites as the main pathway to online news. That describes distribution; it doesn’t tell me whether an individual reader has been deceived. [University of Canberra’s findings](https://www.canberra.edu.au/about-uc/media/newsroom/2026/june/dnr-2026-younger-australians-are-rewriting-the-rules-of-news-consumption).

Bots and coordinated influence require similar precision. ACMA’s September 2025 platform report discusses global disruption of coordinated operations, but says platforms did not report Australia being targeted during the **2024 reporting period**. That is a limit of those reports, not proof that manipulation never happens. Fake-account totals also include spam and scams; they are not a count of political bots persuading Victorian voters. [ACMA’s fourth platform report](https://www.acma.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/Digital%20platforms%20efforts%20to%20combat%20disinformation%20and%20misinformation_4th%20report%20to%20government_0.pdf).

For a conversation with a friend, the useful move is to inspect the claim together. Who produced it? What does the original source say? Would I accept the same evidence if it embarrassed Labor? Disagreement alone tells me none of those things.

<h2 id="questions-before-voting" tabindex="-1">The questions I want answered before voting</h2>

I’m keeping five questions handy:

1. **What actually changed?** A promise, a service, a completed project or a measured outcome?
2. **Who was responsible?** Which government, agency or decision-maker, and over what period?
3. **What supports the claim?** Can we get from the headline to the source and its qualifications?
4. **Who bears the cost?** Including regional communities, volunteers, renters and future taxpayers?
5. **Is the alternative credible?** What would it do differently, with what money, and by when?

The next stage is to apply those questions to **Labor, the Liberals, the Nationals, One Nation, the Greens and relevant independents**. I’ll start with roads and emergency services, then health, housing, household costs, energy and fiscal management. Joint Coalition commitments will need to be distinguished from separate Liberal or National positions. A policy I haven’t verified will be marked as not yet verified; unclear details will remain open questions.

My priorities are becoming clearer: dependable regional services, a fair distribution of costs, competent delivery and government that accepts scrutiny. I’ll have to weigh those against one another, including what can realistically be afforded. Counting a school as one point and an integrity failure as minus one would disguise the judgement involved.

There are things in Labor’s record I value, and failures I want answered for. I haven’t finished deciding what would earn my vote. This is where I’m starting, with the sources open and room to change my mind.
