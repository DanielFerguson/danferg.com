<h2 id="why-im-looking" tabindex="-1">Why I’m looking more closely this time</h2>

I’ve traditionally voted Labor. That’s worth putting on the table before I start assessing a Labor government.

Living in the country has made that vote less comfortable. I’m frustrated by the state of our roads, and by the feeling that regional communities sit a long way down the list while Melbourne gets another enormous project. The emergency-services levy turned frustration into something closer to anger. Asking more of communities that already volunteer and fundraise deserves a proper explanation.

Politics is also turning up in more conversations with friends, family and neighbours, and in more of what I read online. I wanted something more useful to bring to those conversations than whatever headline had annoyed me most recently.

So I started checking. What has actually changed? Which criticisms survive a look at the evidence? Where have I accepted a claim because it suits the way I already vote?

The short version: my frustration about roads survived contact with the evidence and got sharper. My anger about the levy didn’t survive in the form I’d been carrying it around. The government’s record has more in it than its critics allow, and the integrity findings are more serious than its defenders allow, once you sort them by what each body actually found. Each of those has its own article now, because each deserves to be searchable, linkable and updated on its own schedule. This page is the frame that holds them together, and it’s the one I’ll keep sending to people, including people who think I’ve got it wrong.

<GuideMethod summary="How I’m assessing this, and how to read the tags">

Victoria votes on 28 November 2026 <Cite k="vec-election" />. Before crediting or blaming a state government for anything, I’m checking whose job it was. States run public hospitals and schools and make most transport, housing and emergency-service decisions; Canberra controls Medicare and income tax; councils have their own patch; and money crosses all three boundaries <Cite k="peo-levels" />. On roads, the state manages arterial roads and freeways, a little over 23,000 km of them, and councils manage most local roads. Toll roads have their own operators. The Department of Transport and Planning publishes a map of which roads it looks after <Cite k="dtp-road-map" />.

I’m also separating four things that political arguments blur: a promise, money spent, something delivered, and an outcome demonstrated. A hospital opening is real; whether people get timely care needs another measure. Rather than saying that after every fact, the articles tag the facts:

<h2 id="whats-in-the-guide" tabindex="-1">What’s in the guide</h2>

Four pieces look at the government’s record, in the order I worked through them.

[Regional roads: what the audit actually found](/articles/victoria-2026-regional-roads) is where my frustration turned out to have a strong independent foundation. The Auditor-General’s September report puts almost half of state roads, and just over half of regional ones, in the poor or very poor bands for remaining life <Tag kind="independent" />. It also explains why more spending hasn’t yet meant better roads, and what the $352 million added in August does and doesn’t settle.

[The emergency-services levy: why people remember different numbers](/articles/victoria-2026-emergency-services-levy) is where my anger changed shape. Three versions of the farm rate were proposed, cut and frozen inside six weeks, and the increase people quote was real only as a proposal. There’s a calculator for your own property, and the fairness question that survives the arithmetic.

[Labor’s record: the years behind this election](/articles/victoria-2026-labor-record) is what the government deserves credit for, tagged so you can see what was delivered, what’s a government figure and what’s a measured outcome, with a timeline banded by which premier was in charge. It also covers how Ben Carroll came to be Premier in July.

[What the integrity investigations actually found](/articles/victoria-2026-integrity-findings) sorts Watts, Daintree, Richmond, the Games, the pandemic findings, Sandon and the CFMEU review by what was substantiated, whether a corrupt-conduct finding was made, and whether anyone was charged or convicted. Most of the heat in this campaign comes from blurring those distinctions in one direction or the other.

<h2 id="information-and-influence" tabindex="-1">Checking my own information diet</h2>

I came into this worried about News Corp’s influence and about the volume of political material moving through social media. That worry needs the same checking I’ve given everything else, or I’m holding other people’s assumptions to a standard I won’t apply to my own.

The best example is from the levy piece. “Country people now pay 100 to 200% more” started life as a real number: the proposed farm rate was a 189% increase on the old one. It travelled as a percentage, lost the word “proposed”, lost the fact that it applied to the variable part of the bill and not the whole thing, and arrived in conversations as a description of what people are paying now. Nobody had to lie for that to happen. That’s the mechanism I need to watch for, and it doesn’t care which side it helps.

Ownership still matters. News Corp’s titles include the Herald Sun and The Weekly Times, so its editorial choices reach both city and country readers <Cite k="newscorp-brands" />. That makes them relevant without making ownership a verdict on any particular story. Herman and Chomsky’s *Manufacturing Consent* is a useful framework for asking what gets attention, whose account is treated as authoritative and what’s left out <Cite k="manufacturing-consent" />. It establishes nothing about whether a specific Victorian story is false.

Distribution has changed. The University of Canberra’s 2026 Digital News Report found social media has overtaken direct visits to news websites as the main way Australians reach online news <Cite k="dnr-2026" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. That describes the pipe, not whether anyone was deceived by what came through it. On bots and coordinated campaigns, ACMA’s most recent platform report records global takedowns but says the platforms did not report Australia as a target in 2024 <Cite k="acma-4th" />. That’s a limit of the reporting, not proof that manipulation never happens, and fake-account totals include spam and scams rather than political bots persuading Victorians.

For a conversation with a friend, the useful move is the one the whole guide tries to make: inspect the claim together. Who produced it? What does the original source say? Would I accept the same evidence if it embarrassed Labor?

<h2 id="whats-next" tabindex="-1">What’s still to come</h2>

The next stage applies the five questions to Labor, the Liberals under Jess Wilson, the Nationals, One Nation, the Greens and the independents standing where I vote <Cite k="abc-fourth-term" />. [Roads and emergency services](/articles/victoria-2026-roads-emergency-services) came first, because those are the grievances I started with, and [housing and the cost of living](/articles/victoria-2026-housing-cost-of-living), [energy, climate and regional communities](/articles/victoria-2026-energy-climate) [the state budget](/articles/victoria-2026-state-budget) and [what a government that accepts scrutiny would look like in practice](/articles/victoria-2026-integrity-accountability) are now done. Getting care in the country is next, with the people actually on my ballot once nominations close and a final weighing before I vote. Joint Coalition commitments will be kept separate from Liberal-only or National-only positions. A policy I haven’t verified will be marked as not yet verified.

I’ve left crime, debt and hospital waiting times out of the record so far. Not because they don’t matter, but because I haven’t checked them yet and I’d rather say so than pad this out.

My priorities are getting clearer: dependable regional services, a fair distribution of costs, competent delivery, and a government that accepts scrutiny. I’ll have to weigh those against each other, and against what can actually be afforded. Counting a school as one point and an integrity failure as minus one would disguise the judgement involved.

There are things in Labor’s record I value, and failures I want answered for. I haven’t finished deciding what would earn my vote. This is where I’m starting, with the sources open and room to change my mind.
