The energy transition is a city argument that happens in the country. The lines cross paddocks out here, the turbines go up here, the coal jobs are in the Valley, and the offshore wind, if it arrives, arrives off Gippsland. The bills, on the other hand, are paid everywhere. That’s what makes this the topic in [the guide](/articles/victoria-election-2026) where my own interests point in more than one direction at once, and where I most need to separate what I’d like to be true from what the evidence says.

<h2 id="the-same-problem" tabindex="-1">Start with the same problem</h2>

Three clocks are running. Yallourn, one of the Latrobe Valley’s three coal-fired stations, closes in mid-2028, and its owner says it has not discussed an extension with anyone <Cite k="energyaustralia-yallourn" />. The Auditor-General reported in December that the two transmission lines meant to bring replacement power in from the west had slipped, the Western Renewables Link to late 2029 and VNI West to late 2030, and that the first 2 GW of offshore wind will not arrive by the 2032 target <Cite k="vago-renewables-2025" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. And the gas so many Victorian homes heat with is running down: AEMO puts the first peak-day supply risk in the south at around 2029–30, with Victorian production roughly halving by 2030 <Cite k="aemo-gsoo-2026" /> <Tag kind="independent" />.

Against that, the news is better than the argument suggests. Renewables supplied 42.4% of the state’s generation in 2024–25, so the 2025 target was met a year early <Cite k="vret-progress" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />. AEMO’s August outlook forecasts no reliability gaps anywhere in the national market before 2030, after a record 9.1 GW of new generation and storage connected in a year <Cite k="aemo-esoo-2026" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. Emissions were 31.4% below 2005 levels by 2023, inside the 2025 target band, though that is the latest inventory published <Cite k="deeca-emissions-targets" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. The auditor’s worry is the 2030 target, which depends on Canberra’s Capacity Investment Scheme paying out in full and on the lines being built on time <Cite k="vago-renewables-2025" />.

On bills, the regulator’s benchmark fell for once: the default offer for a typical household is $1,591 from July, down $84 or 5% <Cite k="esc-vdo-2026-27" /> <Tag kind="outcome" />. That is a benchmark, not what most people pay, and it says nothing about gas. The last hardship report I could find is two years old and counted about 121,000 households a month more than $300 behind on electricity, a number that was rising even as disconnections fell <Cite k="esc-market-report-2023-24" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. Concession-card holders get 17.5% off <Cite k="dffh-energy-concession" />. The Power Saving Bonus closed in March and nothing has replaced it <Cite k="premier-psb-jan-2026" />.

And then the weather. The January fires burned more than 400,000 hectares and about 300 homes, with a State of Disaster across eighteen council areas <Cite k="risk-frontiers-jan-2026-fires" /> <Tag kind="independent" />. The south-west drought has drawn $146.5 million in state support, with the main grant programs closing at the end of this month <Cite k="agvic-drought" /> <Tag kind="funded" />. Climate policy is abstract until it isn’t.

<h2 id="labors-record-and-offer" tabindex="-1">What Labor has done, and what it’s offering</h2>

The delivered part of the record is bigger than its critics allow and smaller than its ministers say. The revived SEC has a stake in a 600 MW battery that has been operating since December, a solar and battery park under way at Horsham and a wind farm under construction at Delburn <Cite k="sec-news" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. Six renewable energy zones were formally declared in June <Cite k="vicgrid-rez-declared" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. The first offshore wind auction opened on 26 August, for 2 GW, closing next August with contracts in 2028 <Cite k="premier-offshore-auction" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />; the government claims 68,000 transition jobs by 2040, 37% of them regional <Tag kind="govt" />, and the most advanced Gippsland project puts first power at 2032 at best and possibly 2037 <Cite k="abc-star-of-the-south" />. An auction is real. A turbine in the water is not yet.

The gas rules are the part most often misdescribed, in both directions. As they stand, new homes and commercial buildings must be all-electric from 1 January 2027, a failed gas hot-water system must be replaced with electric from 1 March 2027, and rental minimum standards phase in from then to 2030. Gas heating and cooktops in existing homes are not covered, and there are exemptions where compliance is impractical or costly <Cite k="energy-vic-electric-standards" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. That is already the softened version: in June 2025 the Allan government pushed the start back a year, dropped the heating requirement and reserved gas for industry <Cite k="premier-gas-june-2025" />. I found no evidence that Ben Carroll has changed the dates since he became Premier, and no evidence he has confirmed them either.

On transmission, the ground moved today. Carroll paused the Western Renewables Link pending an independent cost assessment to be published by the end of the year, with no compulsory acquisitions in the meantime; the line’s estimate had gone from $479 million in 2019 to $1.53 billion by 2024 <Cite k="aap-wrl-pause" /> <Tag kind="govt" />. That rests on a single wire story as I write, and I’ll update this when the Premier’s office publishes something. It leaves VNI West, the bigger line whose estimate went from $3.9 billion to $7.6 billion and possibly $11.4 billion <Cite k="abc-vni-west-cost-2025" />, officially proceeding and practically in limbo, because it was meant to connect at Bulgana to the line just paused. Landholders on either route are paid $8,000 a kilometre a year for 25 years <Cite k="premier-landholder-payments" /> <Tag kind="funded" />, which is $200,000 a kilometre over the life of the payment <Tag kind="author" />, and which has not made the lines welcome.

What Labor is not offering, so far, is money off bills. The May budget carried no new household energy rebate; the cost-of-living package is the registration rebate and half-price fares covered in [the housing piece](/articles/victoria-2026-housing-cost-of-living) <Cite k="budget-2026-col" />. The government’s bill story is the regulator’s 5% cut and the SEC, and only one of those is its own doing.

<h2 id="the-coalition" tabindex="-1">What the Coalition would change</h2>

The Liberal–National offer is gas and fewer wires. They would repeal the new-home gas ban and oppose the hot-water replacement rule <Cite k="coalition-gas-ban" /> <Tag kind="promise" />, and within 60 days of taking office issue a statewide request for proposals for conventional gas exploration and production <Cite k="coalition-gas-rfp" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. They have ruled out fracking, so the entrenched ban stays <Cite k="bellarine-times-fracking" />. They would scrap VNI West outright and review the Western Renewables Link for value for money <Cite k="abc-coalition-vni-west" /> <Tag kind="promise" />, having said in May they would pause both; repeal the compulsory land-access powers and rescind the fines issued to farmers who refused entry <Cite k="nats-farmer-rights-may-2026" /> <Tag kind="promise" />; and reinstate a 2 km buffer between renewable projects and homes, rebuttable by either side <Cite k="abc-buffer-zones" /> <Tag kind="promise" />.

To keep the lights on after Yallourn they propose an “Energy Security Requirement”: competitive tenders for new firm generation, described as technology-agnostic while the policy text expects gas to provide most of it <Cite k="abc-coalition-firming" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. Loy Yang A and B would run to the end of their technical lives; there would be no new coal <Cite k="abc-coalition-coal" />. Jess Wilson said on her first day as leader that there were no plans to change the legislated targets, while declining to commit personally to net zero by 2045 <Cite k="abc-wilson-first-day" />, and nothing since has repealed or endorsed them.

What I looked for and didn’t find: a dollar figure for what any of this does to a household bill, a megawatt target or cost for the firming tenders, a position on the SEC beyond calling it a fraud, a drought package, a Latrobe Valley jobs plan, or a landholder compensation scheme to replace the one they’d repeal. Their first-100-days plan has one energy line: cancel VNI West and open the door to conventional gas <Cite k="nats-100-days" />. The Clean Energy Council, which is the industry lobby, says the 2 km buffer would make 70% of the state unviable for projects and put $19 million a year in council payments at risk <Cite k="abc-buffer-zones" />; the Coalition calls it balanced. Neither side has shown me the map.

<h2 id="the-crossbench" tabindex="-1">The Greens, One Nation and the paddock</h2>

The Greens’ energy policy is an aims document: 100% renewable electricity by 2030, public ownership of transmission and distribution, publicly owned storage, government-backed loans for household solar and batteries <Cite k="greens-energy-policy" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. The figures attached to it in public, $10 billion for public renewables and about $2.2 billion to get a million homes off gas, are from the Parliamentary Budget Office’s costing of their 2022 platform <Cite k="pbo-greens-2022" /> <Tag kind="independent" />, and I could find no 2026 restatement or costing. Their concrete record is legislative: the constitutional amendments that stop SEC profits being swept into general revenue, and the 2025 VicGrid amendments that expanded the community fund and, with Legalise Cannabis, cut the fine for refusing land access from $12,200 to about $8,000 while the access power itself stayed <Cite k="abc-vicgrid-stage-2" /> <Tag kind="delivered" />. That last one is worth sitting with: the party most committed to the lines is also the one that softened the penalty for standing in front of them.

One Nation launched its energy policy at Loy Yang A on 31 August: keep the plant open past 2035, repeal the 1983 nuclear prohibition, scrap the western renewable zones and the offshore wind zone, reverse net zero <Cite k="abc-onenation-loy-yang" /> <Tag kind="promise" />. AGL restated 2035 the same day, and the party’s state leader said the figures hadn’t been discussed yet. Its written policy adds a preference for undergrounding transmission and a ban on wind and solar on prime farmland, with no cost for either <Cite k="onenation-energy" />. The day before, at Marnoo on the VNI West corridor, it promised farmers their access fines would be wiped if it held the balance of power. That is where this policy is being fought: the Nationals and One Nation are contesting the same paddocks in Ripon, and the farmers’ groups know it <Cite k="abc-farmers-ripon" />. The Victorian Farmers Federation calls VNI West a failed project; the Clean Energy Council asks what the replacement plan is <Cite k="stockandland-vff-vni" />. Both are lobbies. Both are asking a fair question.

The regional independents announced so far have said nothing on energy that I could find, and a crossbench vote worth knowing is the 2024 VicGrid bill, where the Greens, Legalise Cannabis and Animal Justice voted with Labor for the planning powers and the Libertarian, One Nation and several independents voted with the Coalition against them <Cite k="hansard-vicgrid-2024" />. On the other side of the ledger, Friends of the Earth is asking candidates to keep the targets, fund solar for renters and public housing, and attach community benefit packages to the offshore wind regions <Cite k="foe-election-2026" />. It’s the only detailed regional-benefit ask I found from anyone.

<h2 id="who-bears-the-cost" tabindex="-1">Who bears the cost, seen from here</h2>

The person paying the bill and the person hosting the line are usually not the same person, and every party is speaking mainly to one of them. Labor’s offer is to the bill-payer and the Gippsland worker: lines, zones, auctions, and a payment for the paddock. The Coalition’s and One Nation’s offer is to the landholder: no lines, no access powers, no fines. Whether the second offer is also good for the bill-payer depends on arithmetic nobody has published.

Here is the arithmetic I can do. Yallourn closes in 2028. Under Labor’s plan the replacement lines arrive in 2029 and 2030 on the auditor’s reading, and one of them was paused today. Under the Coalition’s plan the replacement is gas from wells that don’t yet have proposals, let alone permits, for generation from tenders with no size or price, in a market where AEMO says southern supply gets tight from 2029 <Cite k="aemo-gsoo-2026" />. Neither side has shown me how 2028 is covered <Tag kind="author" />. AEMO’s answer, for now, is that the national market has enough in the pipeline to get to 2030 without a gap <Cite k="aemo-esoo-2026" />, which is reassuring and not the same as a plan.

On the paddock, the honest position is that a rebuttable 2 km buffer and a repeal of access powers would change the balance of power between a farmer and a proponent, which is what the people asking for them want, and would also make the state’s targets harder to meet, which is what the industry says. Both can be true. What I want from a candidate in a corridor seat is the number the lobbies won’t give me: how many of the projects the state is counting on would the buffer rule out, and where would the replacement power come from.

And the parts that are Canberra’s. The Capacity Investment Scheme the auditor says the 2030 target depends on is federal. The offshore feasibility licences are federal. Any nuclear plant needs a federal law changed first. A state candidate promising any of those is promising to ask.

<h2 id="what-id-need-answered" tabindex="-1">What I’d need answered</h2>

Applying the [five questions](/articles/victoria-election-2026#questions-before-voting). What actually changed: 42.4% renewables and a 5% cut in the benchmark bill are measured; the SEC battery and the declared zones are delivered; the auction is open; the lines are late and one is paused. Who was responsible: the state for targets, zones, gas rules and the SEC; Canberra for the investment scheme and the licences; AEMO and the companies for the lines and the closures. What supports the claim: Labor’s 68,000 jobs and the Coalition’s “downward pressure” are both unshown. Who bears the cost: the landholder now, the bill-payer if the transition stalls, the Valley either way. Is the alternative credible: specific on what it would stop, silent on what it would build, and without a figure for what it would save.

I came into this expecting to side with the farmers on the lines and the government on the targets, and I still mostly do. What the checking added is that those two positions collide in 2028, and that no party has yet told me how it resolves the collision.
