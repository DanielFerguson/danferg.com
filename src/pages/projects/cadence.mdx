## Keeping your eye line near the lens

Every time you record a product walkthrough, film a short update, or run an important client call, you hit the same minor dilemma. Either you try to memorise every point under pressure, or you keep notes off to the side and your eyes visibly drift away from the camera while you talk.

Existing Mac teleprompters solve parts of that problem, though most ask for a recurring subscription, push your speech through a cloud API, or behave clumsily once you are also running Zoom, Loom, OBS, or a pair of AirPods. I wanted something free, native, and completely local on my Mac, and I was curious how far macOS 26's built-in on-device speech stack could go compared with open local streaming models.

Cadence is the result. It is a native Swift 6 macOS app for Apple Silicon that attaches a pitch-black teleprompter directly to your MacBook camera notch, listens locally on your machine, and scrolls your script at your real speaking pace. Speed up when you have momentum, slow down to land a point, or stop to breathe and improvise; the text waits calmly until you rejoin it.

## Living inside the MacBook notch

The placement is the main trick. Because the camera lens sits right inside the MacBook notch, putting the active reading line immediately below that bezel keeps your gaze natural on video. The notch Live Activity interaction was directly inspired by [Moody Teleprompter](https://moodyteleprompter.com/), adapted around local speech tracking and a native script library.

To make the window feel attached to the hardware rather than pasted over the menu bar, the overlay uses a non-activating floating `NSPanel` matched to pure black (`#000000`) with concave top shoulders that melt into the physical notch bezel. Your active reading anchor sits at 28% from the top of the viewport, with spoken text dimmed to 38%, the active line at full contrast, and upcoming lines at 80% so your eye does not hunt for its place.

Motion matters more than I expected in a teleprompter. Bouncy springs feel playful in a launcher, yet distracting when you are reading out loud on camera. Cadence uses non-bouncy, critically damped transitions across its six notch states: a 3-second countdown ring that pre-warms the microphone, active voice tracking, hover hold, manual pause, a compact tucked pill when you want the text out of the way, and full dismissal.

On an external monitor without a notch, or whenever you want to drag the script closer to an external webcam, one click detaches the prompter into a translucent floating window with symmetrical bottom-corner drag-to-resize.

## A script library that behaves like Apple Notes

Most teleprompters treat writing as an afterthought: you draft in Notion or Notes, copy-paste into a bare text box, and immediately lose your edits the next time you tweak a line during rehearsal. I wanted the editor to be somewhere you can actually keep your talks, walkthroughs, and call outlines.

The main workspace borrows Apple Notes' restraint: a three-column native macOS layout with folders, pinned scripts, instant search, live word count and reading-time estimates, and a clean writing canvas. It supports lightweight Markdown cue syntax directly in the editor: `# Section` headings create jumpable cue markers in the notch header, `[pause]`, `[smile]`, and `[breathe]` render as amber stage-cue pills that the voice aligner skips automatically, and `**bold**` emphasises key phrases.

Every script stays in plain UTF-8 Markdown mirrors on disk alongside the local library index, and you can import existing `.txt`, `.md`, or `.rtf` files straight into a folder.

## Drafting and revising scripts from your AI tools via local MCP

A lot of the scripts I want to read on camera start inside another tool: a product walkthrough drafted while looking at a codebase in Cursor, Claude Code, Google Antigravity, or Gemini CLI, or a tight two-minute update shaped in Claude Desktop or ChatGPT. Copying drafts back and forth every time you shorten an intro or add a `[pause]` cue gets old quickly.

Cadence exposes your local script library through the Model Context Protocol (`CadenceMCP`) using two strictly local transports: a bundled native Swift `cadence-mcp` stdio helper (symlinked to `~/.local/bin/cadence-mcp` so paths stay stable and edits still work when Cadence is closed) plus an in-app localhost Streamable HTTP server at `http://127.0.0.1:48621/mcp` bound strictly to loopback with Origin validation. Nothing leaves your machine unless you send it to whatever AI client you are already using.

The server exposes 17 tools covering folder and script management, surgical updates through `edit_script` (find-and-replace, append, or single-section rewrites), `analyze_script` for word-count, pacing, and cue metrics, and `open_script` to highlight a script in the workspace. It also exposes your library as MCP Resources and includes Cadence-native authoring Prompts that already understand `# Section` headings and amber `[stage cue]` pills.

Because letting an external tool edit your speaking notes needs real guardrails, Cadence builds safety into the storage boundary itself:

- **Atomic file locking and live reload:** `Cadence.app` and `cadence-mcp` coordinate reads and writes through POSIX `flock` locks (`ScriptLibraryRepository`) and cross-process notifications, reloading edits straight into the open editor while freezing any active teleprompter session mid-read so your text never jumps while you are speaking.
- **No sudden overlays or permanent deletes:** `open_script` only selects a script in the workspace and never triggers the notch overlay, and delete calls only move scripts to Recently Deleted.
- **20-revision undo safety:** Every AI mutation saves a pre-edit snapshot (keeping the last 20 revisions locally) alongside a recent AI activity strip with one-click Revert.

Setup lives inside a dedicated **AI & MCP** tab in Settings (`⌘,`), a **Connect AI** sheet in the workspace toolbar with a subtle live connection indicator, and a quiet dismissible prompt in the sidebar footer until you connect your first client. Rather than making you hand-edit JSON files by default, Cadence uses native deeplinks for Cursor and VS Code, safe backed-up one-click installs for Claude Desktop (plus `.mcpb` export), Claude Code, Gemini CLI, Google Antigravity, and OpenAI Codex, and copyable JSON/URL cards for ChatGPT Desktop and custom clients, backed by a three-layer check (config status, local server self-test, and real client handshake tracking with a Copy Test Prompt button).

## On-device voice following without the cloud or audio drama

Voice-followed teleprompting fails in two very specific ways in practice. First, speech recognition lags behind or trips over proper nouns, causing the text to stutter or freeze mid-sentence. Second, poorly behaved audio capture grabs exclusive control of the microphone or flips Bluetooth headphones into low-bandwidth hands-free mode right in the middle of a call.

Cadence separates those responsibilities cleanly across Swift 6 modules: `CadenceCore` handles script storage, parsing, phonetic alignment, and scroll physics; `CadenceSpeech` handles passive audio capture and pluggable speech engines; `CadenceMCP` handles local stdio and loopback HTTP tool calls; and `CadenceApp` owns the SwiftUI and AppKit UI.

On the audio side, `AudioCaptureService` attaches a passive, read-only `AVAudioEngine` input tap (also feeding the 5-band Goertzel volume beam in the notch). It does not hijack AirPods routing or interrupt Zoom, Loom, OBS, or Screen Studio.

On the tracking side, partial transcripts feed a three-tier bounded dynamic-programming aligner paired with Double Metaphone phonetic matching and fuzzy fallback. That lets Cadence stay locked onto your place even when a local model mishears an acronym or you drop a connecting word. The aligner outputs a target position into a critically damped 120 Hz scroll controller, while voice-activity detection holds the scroll steady the moment you stop talking. If you prefer steady pacing instead, press `V` anytime to switch to Classic Auto-Scroll between 75 and 240 WPM.

Part of why I built Cadence was wanting to compare today's local macOS speech engines under identical conditions rather than guessing from model cards. Out of the box, Cadence uses Apple's built-in `SpeechAnalyzer` on the Neural Engine with zero download. Inside Settings, you can also download **Moonshine v2 Tiny**, **Moonshine v2 Small** (via [Moonshine Voice](https://github.com/moonshine-ai/moonshine-swift)), or **NVIDIA Parakeet Realtime EOU** (via [FluidAudio](https://github.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio)) with one click and A/B test latency and accuracy on your own scripts, offline.

## Release notes, privacy, and what still has sharp edges

Shipping version 1.0.0 today means being clear about the current boundaries alongside what already works well.

Three trade-offs are worth knowing upfront. First, Cadence targets **Apple Silicon and macOS 26.0+** because it leans directly on Apple's modern on-device `SpeechAnalyzer` APIs and Swift 6 concurrency; Intel Macs and older macOS releases are not supported. Second, ad-hoc signing keeps this initial release free of Apple Developer Program overhead, at the cost of that one-line `xattr` step on first launch. If enough people use Cadence regularly, moving to a notarised Developer ID build is already wired into the release pipeline. Third, hiding an overlay from every screen recorder on modern macOS is less absolute than it used to be, so I have documented the `ScreenCaptureKit` caveat plainly below.

Building Cadence in a day reinforced something I keep seeing with modern local models and native platform APIs: the distance between "this ought to exist as a focused, offline utility" and a working macOS binary has shrunk dramatically. The interesting work now is not wiring up a cloud API; it is sweat-the-details craft around latency, non-intrusive audio routing, eye-line geometry, and calm motion. If you try Cadence on your Mac and find a script style, microphone setup, or speech engine where it stumbles, I would genuinely like to hear about it.
